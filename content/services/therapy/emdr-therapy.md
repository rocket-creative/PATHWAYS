## Understanding EMDR section

**Headline:** There is nothing wrong with you if you struggle to talk about what happened

**Body:**
Trauma can be difficult to process. To truly move past the things you have gone through, it requires reliving them. This is understandably difficult and can be an exhausting process.

If the thought of talking at length about old hurts and hard feelings makes you feel uncertain or overwhelmed with anxiety, EMDR therapy may be the tool you have been looking for.

EMDR is internationally recognized as a beneficial treatment to heal the brain from the residual and lingering effects of trauma. With over three decades of research behind it, Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing is often used to treat PTSD and other trigger based trauma disorders, but that is not all it can do.

## How it works section

**Headline:** What makes EMDR different?

**Body:**
To effectively heal, your brain needs to have a conversation with itself. Three different parts of your brain are involved in that conversation. Respectively, they are responsible for stress response (fight or flight), learning (both making memories and assessing danger), and behavior analysis (what you will do and what others are doing).

When that conversation breaks down, so does your ability to heal from the things that happened in the places it stopped effectively communicating. You freeze, permanently trapped in the alarm state of those events instead of moving through the stages of processing we typically have in response to them.

EMDR works by using repetitive rhythmic stimulation to help push your brain through that freeze so it can organically resume the healing process it has been trapped in.

The stimulation we use is often a repetitive eye movement but may also include things like tapping with your hands or external cues like a tone or sound that is repeated.

This bilateral stimulation is substituted for the time we often spend dwelling on experiences and emotions. Instead, you will briefly recall the trauma you experienced while also experiencing this repeated stimulation. This begins a process of reducing the vividness of your response by allowing your brain to do the work of healing without the need of prolonged emotional exposure to the memory.

## What to expect section

**Headline:** What to expect in EMDR therapy

**Body:**
While the therapeutic experience is not the same for everyone, EMDR treatment is a structured process. Do not worry, there is no one standing over you expecting your response to be as uniform as the guidelines. There is plenty of room to move at your own pace and comfort level.

### Phase 1: History gathering
Introductions and history gathering so we are familiar with one another before we begin.

### Phase 2: Preparation
Getting you ready. It is a priority for us to ensure you always know what to expect.

### Phase 3: Assessment
Assessing what it is you would like to target. We will identify the memory, sticky spot, or trigger to focus on.

### Phases 4 through 7: Active EMDR
These are the active EMDR phases where we use repetitive exposures to create new pathways for your brain to heal. EMDR relies on Adaptive Information Processing, so during these phases, we are focused on rewriting your memory storage.

### Phase 8: Evaluation
Results and evaluation. We will review your treatment together and the results of our work. This gives you the space to determine how you feel now and what comes next.

## Timeline section

**Headline:** Powerfully to the point

**Body:**
If you only have a fixed amount of time or are looking to do focused work on overcoming a single trigger, EMDR is a great choice for your therapeutic tool.

While it can be used alongside or within many therapy modalities, you can begin to see improvement quite quickly without the intensive exposure to your triggers that may have held you back from seeking the support you deserve.

When we are working through a single memory, you can expect to have one to three sessions with your therapist. For prolonged trauma healing, EMDR treatment often lasts for six to twelve sessions that can be done weekly, with some sessions occurring on consecutive days.
